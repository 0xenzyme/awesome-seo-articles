# SEO for Startups: How to Grow Search Visibility on a Budget

Startup SEO is about attracting valuable search traffic by making a startup’s website more visible for relevant keywords on Google and other search engines.

For startups, SEO is worth investing in because it brings in free, organic traffic. Unlike paid ads, this traffic can grow over time and keep delivering results without ongoing costs.

SEO can be expensive if you hire an agency, but it’s also something you can learn and do yourself. It’s not as complicated as it might seem, and you don’t need technical skills like coding to get started. There are plenty of free resources online—including this guide—that can teach you what you need to know.

To make SEO work, startups should follow a few key steps: get team support for the strategy, research the right keywords, create useful content, and build links to their site. There’s also a newer piece to consider—being visible in AI tools like ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, and Google’s AI Overviews. In 2026, SEO isn’t just about Google anymore.

One important thing to keep in mind is that SEO takes time. Sometimes you might see results in a few days, but usually it takes about 3 to 6 months to notice real progress. That’s the trade-off for getting consistent, free traffic—so starting early pays off.

I’ve worked with multiple startups and seen both what works and what doesn’t when it comes to SEO. I’ve also seen how a company can grow into hundreds of millions in revenue with this strategy as a key driver. If you’re curious about SEO, this guide is a solid place to begin.

## Why should startups invest in SEO?

If you still have doubts about [whether SEO is suitable for you](https://ahrefs.com/blog/is-seo-worth-it/) or need some data to support your SEO plan, here are four reasons to do SEO in a startup.

### Reason 1. Because of the macro trend

In 2014, Google and Millward Brown Digital [conducted a study](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/consumer-insights/consumer-trends/the-changing-face-b2b-marketing/) on the research and purchase habits among B2B decision-makers. That study revealed that 71% of B2B researchers start their research with a generic search (instead of a branded search).

As Google itself said, “They’re looking for [a] product first, not for you.”

### Reason 2. Because people are searching for what you do

Even if your product is really niche, people are likely still searching for it in one way or another.

For example, take something like animal-free dairy (real product category). While there may be little search demand for “animal-free dairy” (because this is a new concept), there is tangible demand for related searches like “vegan,” “lactose free,” or “low lactose.”

### Reason 3. Because you can get free organic traffic and scale it

We currently get about 1.1M monthly visits from search engines alone. If we had to buy that traffic using PPC ads, it’d cost us an estimated $2M per month:

So while SEO is never truly free because you have to invest in [content creation](https://ahrefs.com/blog/content-creation/) and optimization, you don’t need to pay for every user and every visit—unlike with ads.

Reason 4. Because AI search runs on the same fundamentals

ChatGPT now has  [900M weekly active users](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/27/chatgpt-reaches-900m-weekly-active-users/), and AI Overviews [show up on 21% of US keywords](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-overview-triggers/). It would be easy to assume Google is dying. It isn’t. According to our data from over 70000 websites, traffic to these sites from AI assistants is 0.33% compared to 34% from traditional search, and that’s mostly Google.

![Traffic share - organic search vs AI assistants](https://ahrefs.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/2026-04-29_11-50-03.png)

What’s more, most people use AI assistants in addition to Google, not instead of it. Which means startups need to win on both surfaces.

But the good news is that 76% of pages cited in AI Overviews [also rank in Google’s top 10](https://ahrefs.com/blog/search-rankings-ai-citations/). The fundamentals (great content, technical health, links, and brand mentions) are still what drive visibility everywhere. So everything in this guide still applies. We’ve just added a Step 9 on the extra moves that improve AI visibility specifically.

## How to do SEO for startups

Follow these ten steps to start doing SEO in your startup the right way.

### Step 1. Get buy-in

Who do you need to convince to start doing SEO in your startup? The CMO, CFO, CEO, investors, your marketing team, or maybe yourself? Whoever it is, it all boils down to improving the bottom line, right?

First of all, SEO is an investment that won’t pay for itself overnight. It takes time to do [keyword research](https://ahrefs.com/seo/keyword-research), create content, and promote it. It even takes time for Google or any search engine to crawl and index your content.

But SEO can pay off unlike anything else, and Ahrefs is living proof of that. We’re a nine-figure ARR company with a [marketing strategy](https://ahrefs.com/blog/marketing-strategy/) built upon SEO content.

If the person you need to convince is interested in marketing, an excellent way to get buy-in is to start with the benefits of SEO.

You can explain the benefits of SEO by illustrating the marketing tactics that it fuels, mainly [content marketing](https://ahrefs.com/blog/content-marketing-for-startups/). Content marketing serves many different purposes:

- Creates awareness and attracts potential customers
- Helps engage your audience and build credibility
- Helps convert customers
- Helps you build loyalty and retention

Content marketing also has compounding results, as I’ve already mentioned.

In other words, if you use SEO to create content, the attribution of SEO will touch every phase of the [marketing funnel](https://ahrefs.com/blog/marketing-funnels/).

### Step 2. Set goals and get resources

If you already have a green light for SEO, it’s time for the next step: set your goals and prepare resources for the trip.

#### How to set SEO goals

The ultimate goal of SEO is to reach prospective users through organic search and convert them into customers. But this is a top-level goal, and it’s quite hard to work with because it doesn’t tell you how you will get there.

A much more efficient approach is to divide your grand, top-level goal into smaller “bites” that can get you closer to the outcome.

You can envision your SEO goals as a pyramid, with the outcome at the top and smaller goals that lead there at lower levels. The idea is to start at the bottom and work your way up.

#### Resources you’ll need

Resources mean costs, and startups need to be careful about them. While SEO doesn’t require any ad budget or super expensive tools, this is not a costless process.

SEO needs these things in order to work:

1. Research
2. Optimized content
3. Backlinks
4. Measurement tools

If you’re a team of one with no budget for building a team or outsourcing, there is no other way besides learning all those things and doing the work yourself on top of anything else you’re currently doing. But no worries. With the right advice, prioritization, and tools, this is doable.

If your startup has a budget for an SEO-related role, here are your options:

- **Hire someone with all the above skills** **–** They are either superstars or people who have owned various marketing operations, possibly in another startup. Superstars will probably be outside your budget, but the latter should be quite possible to find. After all, content marketing and SEO are closely related.
- **Hire someone who has experience in writing and an interest in SEO** **–** Then give them some time to learn SEO on the job. There are many excellent learning materials online, like our completely free [Ahrefs Academy](https://ahrefs.com/academy) or [this very blog](https://ahrefs.com/blog/).
- **Hire someone with some success in SEO and give them the ability to outsource** **–** An experienced SEO professional should be able to create a content strategy. Still, they may need the ability to outsource parts of the process (like content creation or link building) to move fast.

Next stop: tools.

Fortunately for startups, there are a lot of free and freemium tools in the SEO realm. We’ve already gathered a list of the 45 best ones in [this article](https://ahrefs.com/blog/free-seo-tools/).

With SEO tools in a startup, you basically have two options:

1. **Go with free tools (as far as you can)** **–** You can do a lot with [free SEO tools](https://ahrefs.com/free-seo-tools), as I will show you in this guide. The caveat here is that you will have to use different tools with different workflows, and data will always be limited.
2. **Try different premium SEO tools** **–** Once you’ve done so, upgrade to the one that suits you the most. If you include those early in the process of implementing SEO, you’ll have premium data and premium features that will help you (and your team) work more efficiently right from the start.

### Step 4. Find the right keywords

Keyword research is where you find what your target customers are searching for in Google, then analyze and compare those terms to prioritize the best opportunities.

You can learn how to do that in our [beginner’s guide to keyword research](https://ahrefs.com/seo/keyword-research). But as most startups have little to no “authority” and brand equity, we recommend initially targeting low-competition keywords that you can rank for without many backlinks or high website authority.

So, for example, pet insurance startups will likely have a hard time ranking for a keyword like “pet insurance” because the #1 SERP (search engine result page) is dominated by pages with lots of backlinks.

Our Keyword Difficulty (KD) score suggests that you’ll need backlinks from about 932 websites to rank in the top 10:

But within that broad topic, you can find low-competition keywords like “french bulldog pet insurance.”

You can find low-competition keywords at scale using a keyword research tool like Ahrefs’[Keywords Explorer](https://ahrefs.com/keywords-explorer). Just enter a topic there, go to the **Matching terms** report, and set your KD to max 20.

As a result, for a “seed” keyword like “pet insurance,” you’ll get 426 **potential** low-competition keyword ideas you can create content for.

Note that these are your potential keyword ideas because you need to consider a few more factors before finally selecting a keyword—e.g., search intent, competitors’ brand equity, and traffic potential. ### Step 5. Create content

Now that you have an idea of which keywords to target, you need to create high-quality, optimized content that will attract organic traffic, and increasingly, get cited in AI answers. There are a few pieces to this puzzle.

#### A. Make sure you understand your audience

Knowing what people search for online is powerful. However, relying solely on SEO metrics can make you lose focus on the people reading your content. That’s why you sometimes need to broaden your research and tie your SEO findings to your [market research](https://ahrefs.com/blog/market-research/).

The one element of your [go-to market strategy](https://ahrefs.com/blog/building-a-go-to-market-strategy/) that is critical to the selection of topics for your content is your target audience. So make sure that you have your buyer persona ready before creating your first piece of SEO content.

#### B. Design your content with search intent in mind

Search intent refers to what the searcher is looking for. It’s important because Google wants to rank content that’s helpful, so you’re probably not going to rank unless your content aligns with user intent.

To understand the intent behind a keyword, you should analyze the search results for what we call the three Cs of search intent:

1. **Content type** **–** Is there a dominant type of content in the SERP, like blog posts, product pages, videos, or landing pages?
2. **Content format** **–** Is there a dominant content format in the SERP, like guides, listicles, news articles, opinion pieces, or reviews?
3. **Content angle** **–** Is there a dominant angle in the SERP, like freshly updated content or content aimed at beginners?

#### C. Plan, publish, repurpose

At this stage, you probably have dozens of content ideas. How will you manage and create all of that content?

A common practice among professional content creators is keeping a [content calendar](https://ahrefs.com/blog/content-calendar/). It is a system used to organize, manage, and schedule content production.

There is no one way to create a content calendar. First and foremost, a content calendar should be usable for you and your organization. You can use a Google calendar, create a kanban in an app like Notion or Trello, create a project in the likes of Asana, or keep everything in a good ol’ spreadsheet.

- Topic
- Author
- Assignee
- Status
- Due date
- Relevant metadata (business potential, funnel stage, target persona, etc.)
- Format
- Channel

When it comes to writing, recording, and creating content, it is a somewhat personal thing. After all, people have different styles of working and staying creative. Especially in a small startup, tasks tend to have a fair degree of autonomy, so most things will be just “up to you.”

### Step 6. Optimize existing content

Sometimes the best way to get more organic traffic is to optimize your existing content. There are two reasons for this:

1. Your existing content may already be ranking.
2. Your existing content may be the best way to match the search intent.

For example, Google has a pretty clear model of what to rank for the keyword “3d printers”: buying guides. It even ranks guides higher than pages from established 3D printer manufacturers—despite them having more backlinks:

It seems as if most people searching for this term were in buying mode. Thus, Google tries to match that with pages that offer help with buying a 3D printer.

What’s interesting is that one 3D printer manufacturer ranks high among the guides: Formlabs. It has found a way to optimize its existing content to get as far up the SERP as possible by creating a mix of a 3D printer guide and a product category page.

1. Formlabs titled the page “3D Printing **Guide**: Types of **3D Printers**, Materials, and Applications.”
2. They provided some basic definitions, explained the process of 3D printing, showed some types of 3D printers, gave some tips on how to choose one, etc. That’s pretty much everything you’d want from a guide.
3. **But at the same time,** the only 3D printers you see there are Formlabs products. On top of that, they are the first thing you see before you scroll down to the guide section.
4. From a navigational perspective, this is Formlabs’ main product category page. For this reason, anyone who links to the assortment of Formlabs 3D printers will have to link to this page, most probably with some variation of the “3D printer” anchor text.
5. Additionally, this page acts as a [content hub](https://ahrefs.com/blog/content-hub/), spreading “link juice” to the other parts of Formlabs’ website.

And what’s the effect of all that from an SEO perspective?

- The page is optimized for a high “business potential” keyword: “3d printers.”
- Since that keyword has a Parent Topic with higher search volume and the same search intent, “3d printer,” it ranks for that keyword as well.
- The page gets a significant amount of organic traffic from the keyword “3d printers” and even more from the Parent Topic “3d printer,” as you can see in the screenshot below:

### Step 7. Build links

With your SEO content complete, it’s time to move the needle on arguably the most important ranking factor: links. And increasingly, brand mentions.

#### Link internally

Let’s start with something easy: linking between different pages on the same website.

Here’s what happens when you link internally:

1. **You help new pages get discovered by search engines.** This is because it provides a crawl path to the new page.
2. **You pass link authority between your pages.** This way, you can boost your underperforming pages.
3. **You help search engines understand what a page is about.** This is important because you need Google to understand the meaning of your content if you want to rank for relevant keywords.

To find relevant internal link opportunities, check the**Link opportunities** report in [Ahrefs Webmaster Tools](https://ahrefs.com/webmaster-tools). It shows unlinked keywords that could be used as links to another page.

For example, from a blog post about rank trackers, AWT suggests that we could link the keyword “keyword research” to a list of “keyword research tools.”

There are a few more good practices of internal linking. We’ve gathered the most important ones in [Internal Links for SEO: An Actionable Guide](https://ahrefs.com/blog/internal-links-for-seo/).

#### Analyze your competitors’ links

Unless you’re in some kind of “unheard of” blue ocean market, there is someone who has been blazing the trails before you. In other words, another company has already done its keyword research, created content, and built links to its website.

By now, your competitors may be a few steps ahead of you, but the good news is that you can take up where they left off and steal some of their thunder. Sounds interesting?

This link-building strategy is all about analyzing your competitors’ links to learn two things:

1. **Links that you can replicate** **–** Some of your competitors’ links may come from sources that should probably link to you too, like lists of the best apps, reviews, etc. You can pitch to those sources and get featured and linked.
2. **Patterns in the types of backlinks** **–** You can study what [types of links](https://ahrefs.com/blog/types-of-backlinks/) helped your competitors rank and see which strategies and tactics are likely to work for you. For example, you may find that a competitor has built several backlinks by creating a unique study that other sites quote and link to.

#### Get reviews and YouTube mentions

If there’s one thing the data is clear on for AI visibility, it’s this: YouTube mentions correlate with ChatGPT brand visibility more strongly than [any other factor we measured](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-brand-visibility-correlations). Two reasons: OpenAI’s GPT-4 was trained on over a million hours of YouTube transcriptions, and when a prompt asks for video content, LLMs reach for YouTube first.

For a startup with a tight budget, this matters more than almost anything else you can do. Two ways to get YouTube mentions:

- - **Publish your own videos**. Even a small channel that ranks for a handful of relevant terms creates training data and citation surfaces.

- **Sponsor or appear on existing channels**. Relevant podcasts, niche review channels, and creator videos. You don’t need a Mr. Beast deal. Small, on-topic creators are often a better fit and far cheaper.

And while you’re at it, the classic review play still works.

Getting your product reviewed online has obvious benefits: brand and product awareness, incoming traffic, and, often, valuable product feedback. But there’s another “silent hero” in that story: backlinks.

Let’s say you’re a tech startup making electric scooters. Here are two ideas on how to look for review sites:

Plug your competitor’s URL in Ahrefs’ [Site Explorer](https://ahrefs.com/site-explorer), go to **Backlinks** report, and filter the results for referring URLs that contain words like “review,” “guide,” or “best.” This will show you websites that have reviewed your competitor. From there, you can browse through the results and pitch your product to websites that stand out to you.

One piece of advice here: Don’t ask for a review only to get a backlink. Above all, think about how the review can influence your product’s sales, and do it only if your product is ready. Backlinks are just a bonus in this game.

#### Digital PR (and unlinked brand mentions)

Getting your business mentioned by the press is great in itself. But in addition to that, you can boost your rankings through links included in the story.

A new wrinkle in the AI era: even unlinked brand mentions matter. We found that [branded web mentions correlate strongly with how often ChatGPT brings your brand up](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-brand-visibility-correlations/). LLMs look for consensus. When many sources mention you in the right context, you start showing up in AI answers, with or without a backlink. So a podcast appearance, a Reddit thread, or a quote in a roundup post all count, even if no one links to you.

Startups often possess that novelty factor, which makes them great candidates for press coverage. Here’s an example story from The Next Web on a startup called Sprig that creates an AI-powered tool never seen before (naturally, it features a link to the company).

Some of the links you will get this way will be “[nofollow](https://ahrefs.com/blog/nofollow-links/)” and likely won’t pass “link authority,” but that shouldn’t dissuade you. Even if a link doesn’t directly boost rankings, chances are one story can lead to mentions on other websites; on top of giving you additional recognition, these can boost your rankings.

Here are a few more tips on getting your story covered by the press (and getting a link):

- **Answer journalist requests** on sites like [HARO](https://www.helpareporter.com/), [ResponseSource](https://www.responsesource.com/), [ProfNet](http://www.prnewswire.com/profnet/), or [SourceBottle](http://www.sourcebottle.com/). You can also scan Twitter for hashtags like #journorequest. This way, you can get quoted and request to include a link in your bio.
- **Write and pitch press releases** to reputable and relevant media outlets. Don’t overdo it, or else you will be ignored the next time you pitch something. Also, focus on newsworthy facts. You can probably get coverage more often in industry magazines, so you can try pitching to these kinds of sites on more occasions.
- **Try to include something “link-worthy”** in your pitch, like unique data. Don’t forget high-quality images.

### Step 8. Build for AI search visibility

The goal isn’t to “rank #1” though, since AI responses are non-deterministic. It’s visibility: the percentage of relevant prompts where your brand gets mentioned and your pages get cited.  And here are the five things that startups should focus on to increase their AI visibility and manage their brand reputation in AI-generated answers.

#### Build mentions across the web

The biggest single lever. After analyzing 75,000 brands, we found [brand mentions correlate with AI visibility far more strongly than DR or backlinks](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-brand-visibility-correlations/). YouTube mentions topped the list (~0.737), followed by general branded web mentions (0.66–0.71). Backlinks barely registered.

For a startup, this is good news. You can build mentions faster than you can build DR. Get on YouTube (your own channel or sponsorships on niche creators), pitch yourself to “best of” lists and comparison roundups, and show up on Reddit and podcasts. Even unlinked mentions count.

Want to know where AI is mentioning your competitors but skipping over you? Run a mention gap analysis every month or two.

In [Ahrefs Brand Radar,](https://ahrefs.com/brand-radar) add your domain plus 3–5 competitors, then click “Others only” on the AI charts—that filters down to prompts where competitors come up, and you don’t. From there, open the Cited Pages report to see which third-party pages AI is actually pulling answers from. Each of those pages is a real outreach target—get mentioned there, and you have a shot at showing up in the same AI answers your competitors do.

#### Keep your brand information consistent everywhere

AI pulls from many sources, and when they contradict each other, the model picks one (often not the one you want) or blends them into something wrong.

Pick one canonical version of your name, tagline, founding year, and one-liner. Audit your profiles on LinkedIn, Crunchbase, G2, Capterra, ProductHunt, AngelList, Wikipedia. Add the Organization schema with ‘sameAs‘ to your social profiles. Make sure founder profiles match across the web.

In Brand Radar, search for your brand and open the AI responses tab, then pick the AI index you want to look at (ChatGPT, Gemini, etc.).

Skim the responses for things like the wrong address or phone number, old taglines, outdated product descriptions, or negative framing. If you’re seeing thousands of responses, filter down—queries containing your brand, queries about a specific topic, or queries with a competitor’s name all surface different patterns. When something’s off, note the query and the cited source so you know exactly where to go fix the original.

- [How to Audit Brand Mentions for Modern SEO](https://ahrefs.com/blog/audit-brand-mentions/)

#### Be transparent and publish your own facts

AI hates information gaps. If you don’t publish something, it guesses, and it’ll trust someone else’s guess over your silence.

We tested this with a [made-up brand experiment](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-vs-made-up-brand-experiment/) where we planted three contradicting third-party sources about a fake company. Most AI models believed the fictions over the brand’s official FAQ. One invented an entire Black Friday sales analysis from thin air rather than admit it didn’t know. When forced to choose between vague truth and specific fiction, AI usually picks the specific fiction.

Defend yourself by (over)publishing your own facts: a detailed FAQ with direct denials of rumors (“we have not been acquired”), a “how it works” page, pricing in plain text, original data and benchmarks, comparison pages framing yourself against competitors. Specific, dated, structured.

#### Keep your docs and help center current

This one bites SaaS startups specifically. When someone asks an AI assistant about your product, the model often grounds its answer in your documentation, help center, and changelog pages. They’re well-indexed and authoritative for your brand, so they’re the easiest source to pull from.

If those docs are out of date, the AI confidently repeats stale information: pricing from a 2-year-old comparison page, deprecated features, missing integrations that shipped six months ago. Your prospect reads that, and you’ve lost them before they ever hit your website.

For a fast-moving startup, this is real exposure. Audit your docs quarterly, update public changelogs (AI weights recency), and once a month spot-check what ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Gemini say about you on common questions. When the answer is wrong, find the source it’s pulling from and fix it.

Brand Radar’s Cited Pages report tells you exactly which of your pages are being read.

#### Create content AI wants to cite

AI assistants disproportionately cite a few formats: how-to guides, comparison pages (“X vs Y”), data studies with original numbers, “best of” lists, and glossary pages. Structure them for \*extraction\*: lead each section with a one-sentence answer (BLUF), use H2s phrased as questions, keep declarative facts in short paragraphs so AI can lift them cleanly. FAQ schema where it fits.

Two small details that punch above their weight:

- [Natural-language URL slugs get cited 89.78%](https://ahrefs.com/blog/why-chatgpt-cites-pages/) of the time vs 81.11% for opaque ones.
- [AI cites content that’s 25.7% fresher than what ranks organically](https://ahrefs.com/blog/do-ai-assistants-prefer-to-cite-fresh-content/). Schedule real refreshes on your top pages. Don’t fake it with date changes.

### Step 9. Track and measure

Finally, how do you measure success in SEO? What metrics should you focus on?

That all ties into the goals you’ll set, which I explained in step two of this guide.

Depending on your goals, the SEO metrics you’ll be tracking most frequently are:

1. **Organic traffic** **–** The number of visits you get from the SERPs. You can measure this using website analytics software like Ahrefs’ [Web Analytics](https://ahrefs.com/web-analytics), Google Analytics or Google Search Console.
2. **Organic keywords** **–** The keywords that your webpages rank for. Some website analytics tools (e.g., Google Analytics) will hide some of the keywords under the “not provided” category, while others (like Google Search Console) will show a limited number. A tool like [AWT](https://ahrefs.com/webmaster-tools) will fix that for you.
3. **Keyword rankings** **–** Where you rank for tracked keywords in the SERPs over time. For a basic, cost-free solution, head over to our [free keyword rank checker tool](https://ahrefs.com/keyword-rank-checker).
4. **Backlinks** **–** Links to your website from other websites. As I already mentioned, backlinks are one of the most important ranking factors. For tracking up to 100 backlinks to any website, a [free backlink checker tool](https://ahrefs.com/backlink-checker) by Ahrefs will do the trick.
5. **AI share of voice**. Out of all the AI responses for your topic or category, what percentage actually mention your brand? It’s the cleanest way to track whether AI sees you as a real player in your space or an afterthought. Set up [some AI visibility prompts](https://ahrefs.com/blog/custom-prompt-tracking/) and watch the AI share of voice month over month against your top competitors. If their share is climbing and yours is flat, that’s your signal to invest in the things AI tends to cite—original data, expert quotes, and pages that get linked from third-party sites. [Brand Radar gives you this number out of the box](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-search-competitor-analysis/#section5).

### Step 10. Expand globally with international SEO

If your startup is seeing traction in one market, expanding internationally through SEO can unlock huge growth potential. International and multilingual SEO can help you reach audiences in new countries or those who speak different languages, even within your existing market.

For example, top global companies like Canva, Wise, and Amazon generate over half their organic traffic from multilingual content. Canva alone saw a 164% lift in organic traffic by targeting multiple languages and dialects with tailored design and content strategies.

Here are some tips to get started:

1. **Start language-first if you’re low on resources.** You don’t have to launch full-blown country-specific sites. A language-led strategy (e.g. English + Spanish content) is easier to manage and can still open doors globally.
2. **Use subfolders or subdomains.** Subfolders like `/es/` or `/fr/` are often best for startups because they consolidate your domain authority, making SEO more efficient than managing multiple ccTLD.
3. **Localize beyond translation.** Translate content, yes, but also adapt tone, images, and even color schemes to match cultural preferences. Canva does this well, offering French users elegant designs while showcasing bold, vibrant templates to Mexican audiences.
4. **Do international keyword research.** Terms vary by country and dialect, even in the same language. Use tools like Keywords Explorer to compare volume and competition in each target market. For example, Australians search for “bin chicken,” not “ibis bird”.
5. **Implement [hreflang tags](https://ahrefs.com/blog/hreflang-tags/).** These help Google serve the right version of your content to the right user. Avoid automatic redirects based on IP—it confuses both users and search engines.
6. **Build local backlinks.** Your authority needs to grow in every target market separately. Getting featured on local sites helps boost rankings regionally.

## FAQ

Before we wrap things up, let’s answer a few common questions about doing SEO in a startup.

### Why do startups need SEO?

Because startups need scalable growth tactics that can be done with little or no money. SEO is a great fit because it doesn’t need any ad budget, and it has compounding effects.

SEO has even more benefits for startups, as explained in the first chapter of this guide.

### How much does SEO cost?

It depends on whether you want to do it yourself or hire someone for it. Here are your options:

- **Hiring an agency** **–** Probably the most expensive option, costing $134.66/hour (+ retainer) on average. But you don’t have to learn SEO, and you can start fast.
- **Building a team or outsourcing to freelancers/consultants** **–** Somewhat expensive. On average, SEO consultants cost $122.33/hour (+ retainer), freelancers cost $68/hour (+ retainer), and an in-house SEO specialist costs $71K/year (in the U.S.). You need to know what you’re doing, and you need to invest time in the hiring and onboarding process.
- **Doing it yourself** **–** The cheapest option. But it takes time and skill.
- **Hybrid option** **–** hiring an agency/freelancer for some time to see the results. Then you may want to build an in-house SEO team or even learn SEO yourself.

The cost estimates are based on our [study](https://ahrefs.com/blog/seo-pricing/).

### Can I do SEO by myself?

If you run a small to medium-sized website, the short answer is yes. All you need is some time to learn and practice SEO, plus good SEO tools.

For bigger sites, one SEO specialist on the job may not be enough.

The main thing to consider here is whether you have the time to learn and actually do SEO for your website. Alternatively, you can hire someone for SEO; see the average prices listed above.

### Is SEO difficult to learn?

The [basic process of SEO](https://ahrefs.com/seo/seo-basics) is not complicated. You can start creating search-optimized content after reading/watching one good guide on the topic.

Naturally, that won’t make you an SEO pro yet. As with all things in life, it takes knowledge and practice to get the best possible results.

### Where can I learn SEO for free?

There are many options online. You can start from our guide on [SEO for beginners](https://ahrefs.com/seo) or take a free course in our [Academy](https://ahrefs.com/academy).

### Is coding required for SEO?

Coding is not required for SEO. That said, there are some areas of SEO where basic coding skills can help you get work done faster - [here are some examples](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QMk2iaJGpSA).
