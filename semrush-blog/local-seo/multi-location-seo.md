# Multi-location SEO: How to build a scalable strategy

The more locations you manage, the more easily small SEO problems multiply.

An outdated Yelp business listing or an incorrect Google Business Profile (GBP) is easy to fix once. But across dozens of locations, the same issue is much harder to correct.

A multi-location SEO strategy solves this with repeatable processes for managing data, pages, profiles, reviews, and performance while preserving what makes each location locally relevant.

This guide shows you how to build that system, including what to standardize, what to keep local, and where to invest more heavily as you scale.

## What is multi-location SEO?

Multi-location SEO is the practice of improving local search visibility for a business that operates across more than one geographic market. This applies to chains, offices, stores, clinics, service-area businesses, and franchises. For franchise networks, this falls under [franchise SEO](https://www.semrush.com/blog/franchise-seo/), a type of multi-location SEO focused on improving visibility across individual franchise locations.

A standard SEO strategy typically focuses on improving a website's overall visibility. Multi-location SEO adds a geographic layer: each location may need its own page, Google Business Profile, local keywords, reviews, listings, and performance tracking.

Each location also presents a slightly different SEO problem. The search demand, competitors, reviews, and local visibility can all change from one market to another.

The scale of the work makes duplication tempting, but seldom effective. The challenge is keeping the parts of your strategy that should be consistent under control without treating every location as interchangeable.

## Why does multi-location SEO get harder as you scale?

Multi-location SEO gets harder as you scale because every new location adds more business data, pages, profiles, reviews, and rankings to keep accurate and locally relevant.

Take [Sam The Concrete Man](https://samtheconcreteman.com/).

The franchise operates more than 100 locations, each competing in its own local market. Their marketing team has to keep franchise information accurate [across more than 70 directories](https://www.semrush.com/company/stories/samtheconcreteman/) while monitoring SEO performance across the network.

The brand SERP for Sam The Concrete Man also shows how location-level discovery can surface inside a broader brand search: Google displays “Locations” and “Knoxville” refinement chips, plus a Knoxville-specific People Also Ask question.

![Google results for Sam the Concrete Man showing location-related search refinements and a Knoxville question](https://static.semrush.com/blog/uploads/media/ad/17/ad178350570f9744f70dbc3b96aceb59/c545d7b1e4ce9f9974e8ee37daa837c7/image.png)

At 100+ locations, doing good SEO once is only half the job. The other half is making sure it gets done consistently across every location.

These problems tend to show up in four places:

- **Business information**: Hours, phone numbers, services, and other details fall out of sync across the website, Google Business Profiles, and listings
- **Location pages**: Reused templates leave branches with thin or near-duplicate pages instead of information specific to the local market
- **Profile ownership**: Your access to Google Business Profiles gets fragmented across corporate teams, agencies, franchisees, or individual employees
- **Reviews**: Some locations accumulate recent customer feedback while others build much weaker review profiles

The more locations you add, the more often the same SEO task has to be repeated, checked, and kept consistent across different markets.

## 1. Create one source of truth for every location

For every location, create one authoritative record that feeds the pages, profiles, and listings you need to keep in sync.

Your central database might live in a spreadsheet, content management system (CMS), or customer relationship management (CRM) platform.

At minimum, record:

- **Identity**: Business name, location ID or [store code](https://support.google.com/business/answer/4542487?hl=en), and operational status
- **Contact details**: Address, phone number, and website URL
- **Hours**: Regular hours, holiday hours, and temporary closures
- **Location page**: The canonical URL for that branch
- **Services**: Products or services available at that specific location
- **GBP categories**: Primary and secondary Google Business Profile categories
- **Coordinates**: Latitude and longitude for the physical location
- **Ownership**: The team or role responsible for maintaining the record

Treat the central database as the canonical version. Your location page, Google Business Profile, [LocalBusiness structured data](https://www.semrush.com/blog/local-business-schema/), and external listings should all reflect it.

If a branch changes its Saturday hours, for example, update the master record first. Then push or verify the change everywhere that consumes that information.

Otherwise, you can end up correcting one platform while another team continues distributing the old hours.

Also document who owns each part of the process. Decide who can change source data, who approves high-impact updates such as closures or relocations, and who checks that those changes reached the systems downstream.

A single source of truth only works if there’s also a single process for changing it.

## 2. Research search demand for each market

Research search demand independently for the markets you serve rather than assuming the same keyword set applies everywhere.

The core service may remain the same, but the language around it may differ by city, neighborhood, or region. The location modifiers, the service names, or the qualifiers may vary from one market to the next.

And even when two markets use the same query, the search results can feature different competitors and page types.

Start with combinations such as:

- **Service + city**: “[service] in [city]”
- **Service + neighborhood**: “[service] [neighborhood]”
- **Local terminology**: Regional names or phrases customers use for the same service
- **“Near me” searches**: Queries such as “[service] near me” and more specific variations
- **Service-specific modifiers**: Queries that narrow by product, audience, urgency, or use case

Run the same service query in two cities, and you can see the difference.

Search “plumbers in New York City,” for example, and Google surfaces Yelp, Bark, Yellow Pages, and ThreeBestRated in its “Places sites” results.

![Google results for “plumbers in New York City” showing Places sites and a local business map pack](https://static.semrush.com/blog/uploads/media/ab/b0/abb06319ce10c1c4ff23826f366927a5/1a0d9fe1d6aa3c9a7fd152c7057311b6/image.png)

Search “plumbers in houston texas,” and Bark drops out while Craigslist appears. The third-party sites worth maintaining can vary by market. A directory that’s important for one location may be less relevant for another, which is why listing priorities should be set location by location.

![Google results for “plumbers in Houston Texas” showing local listings and Craigslist in the Places sites carousel](https://static.semrush.com/blog/uploads/media/57/8d/578dcd19e417583b96764c677e7aa12e/7f8115f8ce8742ab1a930f36ed653f53/image.png)

A multi-location business needs [local keyword research](https://www.semrush.com/blog/local-keyword-research/) at the location level. You’re looking for which terms people use, which competitors appear, and what kinds of pages Google surfaces for those searches.

Use Semrush’s [Keyword Magic Tool](https://www.semrush.com/analytics/keywordmagic/) to expand a seed term into related queries and group them by topic, so you can see how search demand breaks down within a market.

For “plumbers in new york city,” the topic groups surface searches around the Bronx, Queens, Brooklyn, Manhattan, and East Flatbush.

![Semrush Keyword Magic Tool showing the “new york plumbers” topic with keyword volume and difficulty data](https://static.semrush.com/blog/uploads/media/08/d0/08d02377523a918bb0212f29fd76e9d6/c3260d8817e0f01ae64cb7c1a7146289/image.png)

The topic groups tell you the city-level query is only part of the picture. For a multi-location business, the submarket clusters can help you decide whether a location should target the broader city, a specific borough or neighborhood, or a more specialized service query.

### Create a location-to-keyword map

A location-to-keyword map assigns each important search cluster to the location or service page that should own it.

The map should make two things obvious: who owns the demand and where the opportunity is strongest.

Start with the clusters you found in Keyword Magic Tool. For each one, record:

- **Location**: The branch or service area the keyword cluster relates to
- **Primary query cluster**: The main group of searches the page should target
- **Supporting queries**: The closely related terms and modifiers that belong with the same intent
- **Target URL**: The page responsible for satisfying that demand
- [**Keyword difficulty (KD%)**](https://www.semrush.com/kb/1158-what-is-kd): The relative ranking difficulty and opportunity for the cluster
- **Overlap**: Other pages targeting substantially the same searches

In the New York plumbing example, the question is which page should own each level of geographic demand.

The broad 1.4k-keyword “new york plumbers” topic belongs to an NYC hub page, while borough topics belong to their respective branch pages. A narrower topic such as “east flatbush plumbers” can support the Brooklyn page rather than creating another URL targeting closely related searches.

## 3. Build a scalable location-site architecture

A scalable location-site architecture gives every branch a clear path from the main site, even as the number of locations grows.

For most multi-location brands, keep location pages in subfolders on the main domain, using a structure such as /locations/state/city/. The uniform URL structure puts every branch inside the same site architecture and lets internal links and authority flow through one domain. Sometimes franchise brands let individual franchisees run their own microsites, but that can split SEO signals and make business information, content, and technical standards harder to keep consistent.

A single domain is usually easier to scale and govern unless there's a strong operational reason for separate location websites.

Start with a central “Locations” hub on your website that links to every branch. IHOP, for example, includes “Locations” in its main navigation and then lets customers browse restaurants by state from the hub page.

![IHOP locations hub page listing restaurant locations by U.S. state](https://static.semrush.com/blog/uploads/media/20/e4/20e48a754026ff728f4b4b16049c226a/4d6aea178e1df5fab8e570e987380ce1/image.png)

If the list gets too long to scan comfortably, add state or regional pages where they help people navigate. IHOP does this too: its main Locations hub leads to state-level pages, such as Alaska, which then sit between the national hub and individual restaurant pages.

![IHOP Alaska location page with location-focused heading, breadcrumb navigation, and social links](https://static.semrush.com/blog/uploads/media/f1/4a/f14a0139426893823818c1e3a3f7d00e/e84b49057a6890c24b1b05ed5eb0a409/image.png)

For businesses without storefronts, an “Areas We Serve” hub can play the same role.

Make sure every individual location page is reachable through crawlable internal links, meaning search engines can follow a link to discover and access the page. Store locators are a common failure point. If location pages only appear after a visitor enters a ZIP code or uses an interactive search tool, search engines may not have standard <a href> links to follow.

[Google recommends](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/links-crawlable) that every page you care about has a link from “at least one other page on your site.” Use standard [<a href> links](https://www.semrush.com/blog/ahref-link/) that Google can crawl.

### Connect location pages with service pages

Link each location page to the services available there, and link service pages back to the locations that provide them.

For example, if a clinic offers orthodontics at every branch but pediatric dentistry at only four, every location page can *link* to orthodontics. But the pediatric dentistry page should point only to the four clinics where that service is available.

The two-way linking helps customers move between what they need and where they can get it:

- **Location pages**: Show which services are available at that branch
- **Service pages**: Show which locations offer that service

Nearby-location links can help too, but only where they solve a real customer problem. For instance, a nearby branch with Saturday hours or a service the current location doesn’t offer is good information to have.

### What if you have several locations in the same city?

If you have several locations in the same city, give each eligible location its own page and Google Business Profile. Then differentiate each one with local details like neighborhood, services, staff, and customer proof.

The urgent care chain CityMD, for example, has several Manhattan locations, each with its own Google Business Profile and position on the map.

![Google results for CityMD Manhattan showing three urgent care locations mapped across Manhattan](https://static.semrush.com/blog/uploads/media/25/f4/25f4b07b4611670eebb7d29c9aaf9a0b/e9b55b8d88a2b5e41bfd59b249658f73/image.png)

At this step, the location-to-keyword map from the previous section helps. If two branches are assigned the same broad query cluster, decide which page is the better fit, and give the other a more specific neighborhood or service focus.

[Google says](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en) local results are mainly shaped by relevance, distance, and popularity. So, several branches of the same business can surface differently depending on where the searcher is located.

[Semrush’s Map Rank Tracker](https://www.semrush.com/map-rank-tracker/) lets you see that variation across a geographic grid. Run the same local keyword for each branch to see where one location’s visibility starts to drop and another’s picks up. We'll come back to Map Rank Tracker in Step 12.

## 4. Create a useful page for every real location

Give every real location its own page with the details a customer needs before choosing that branch: address and phone number, hours, services, staff, directions, photos, reviews, and a clear way to call or book. Then add what only that branch can say, such as entrances and parking, neighborhoods served, local FAQs, or events hosted there.

A location page should help someone understand what the location offers and what they need to know before choosing it.

***Further reading****:* [*Location Page SEO: How to Create Optimized Location Pages*](https://www.semrush.com/blog/location-page-seo/)

### Standardize the template instead of the local information

Standardize the page structure across locations, but let the information inside each module change with the branch.

You might reuse the same modules for NAP, hours, services, staff, reviews, directions, photos, FAQs, and offers. A consistent template makes hundreds of pages easier to maintain and correct, if needed.

Swap only the city name, address, and title, and the pages may still read like copies of one another.

[Semrush’s Site Audit](https://www.semrush.com/siteaudit/) flags pages as duplicate content when it finds [85% similarity](https://www.semrush.com/kb/119-duplicate-content).

A shared template can contribute to that similarity. In Semrush, pages with only one or two sentences of unique body copy may be flagged when the same header, footer, and other template elements make up most of each page.

For a large location directory, run Site Audit on the location subfolder and review duplicate-content issues together. Site Audit can crawl a specific subfolder and includes duplicate-content checks among more than 140 technical and on-page checks.

But templating can also go too far. IHOP’s Alaska location page, for example, largely reuses the copy from its national locations hub with “in Alaska” substituted into the text. The page is technically location-specific, but the underlying content adds little information that’s unique to Alaska.

Google’s concern goes beyond literal duplication. [Google’s scaled content abuse policy](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) warns against creating large numbers of unoriginal pages primarily to manipulate search rankings. For multi-location sites, avoid spinning up near-identical location pages that add little value beyond a changed place name.

### What should be unique on each location page?

Each location page should contain information that could only truthfully belong to that branch.

The location page information can include:

- **Services and availability**: What this location offers, including services unavailable elsewhere
- **People**: Staff members, practitioners, or specialists based there
- **Customer evidence**: Reviews, project examples, or testimonials from local customers
- **Photos**: Images of the actual branch, team, facilities, or completed work
- **Getting there**: Parking, public transport, entrances, accessibility, or nearby landmarks
- **Local coverage**: Neighborhoods or communities the branch genuinely serves
- **FAQs**: Questions customers regularly ask that particular location
- **Offers or events**: Promotions, classes, events, or other activity specific to the branch

A rule of thumb: if you swapped the city name for another location, would the information still be true? If most of the page survives unchanged, there probably isn't enough local substance yet.

## 5. Avoid doorway pages when targeting additional cities

Avoid doorway pages by creating geographic pages only when the business genuinely operates in the area, and the page can help customers there.

A doorway page is a page created mainly to rank for a specific search or location and funnel visitors toward the same destination as other similar pages.

We're talking about three different page types here:

- **Location page**: Represents a real branch customers can visit, with its own address, hours, staff, services, and other location-specific information
- **Service-area page**: Represents a market the business serves without necessarily having a storefront there
- **Doorway page**: Targets a geographic query but offers little distinct value beyond changing the place name

Google classifies pages targeted at different regions or cities that funnel users to one page as “[doorway abuse](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies#doorways).”

Therefore, search volume alone isn’t a reason to create a city page.

A cleaning company that regularly serves San Francisco and has San Francisco-specific projects, reviews, and service information has something unique to say on a San Francisco page. If the same company creates 40 near-identical city pages and only changes the place name, those pages add very little for customers or search engines.

### When should you create a service-area page?

Create a service-area page when there is meaningful local search demand, and you have enough local information to make the page useful on its own.

Lock in three elements before creating a service-area page:

- **Search demand**: Relevant city, neighborhood, or regional queries appear in your keyword research
- **Real service coverage**: The business routinely accepts and fulfills work in that area
- **Local substance**: You can include relevant services, projects, customer evidence, neighborhoods served, FAQs, or other information specific to the market

## 6. Optimize Google Business Profiles for every location

[Optimize your Google Business Profile](https://www.semrush.com/blog/google-business-profile-optimization/) for every eligible location, using accurate information for that specific branch.

Keep each profile current with:

- **Business name**: Use the real-world name customers see on signage and other branding
- **NAP**: Keep the location’s name, address, and phone number accurate
- **Hours**: Update regular, holiday, and temporary hours
- **Categories**: Choose the most specific category that describes the business, plus only relevant secondary categories
- **Services**: List the services customers can actually get at that branch
- **Photos**: Use current images of the location, team, products, or services
- **Website**: Link to the corresponding location page rather than sending every branch to the homepage
- **Conversion links**: Add booking, ordering, reservation, or other action links where relevant

[Google recommends](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en) providing a phone number or website that represents the individual business location.

Sam The Concrete Man, for example, maintains separate Google Business Profiles for individual franchise locations. The Frisco North Corridor, Mississauga, and North Chicago locations each have their own phone number and business hours, so customers can reach the right branch directly.

![Google results for Sam the Concrete Man showing multiple business locations with phone numbers and hours](https://static.semrush.com/blog/uploads/media/f1/ab/f1ab7e2d937f78059d5fa5e029fd0fa4/92082bbbc4f7722c2e2a272f0ebc88d3/image.png)

### Manage Google Business Profiles at scale

To manage Google Business Profiles at scale, use Google’s bulk-management and access controls instead of assigning profiles informally to individual employees.

[Businesses with 10 or more locations](https://support.google.com/business/answer/4490296?hl=en) can use Business Profile Manager to add, verify, and manage profiles in bulk.

[Google also recommends using Business Groups](https://support.google.com/business/answer/6085339?hl=en) when multiple people need access to a set of profiles. A Business Group works like a shared folder, allowing owners and managers to manage profiles without sharing one Google account or password.

Google recommends limiting Business Groups to one per business or brand. Separate groups are more useful when different brands or divisions need different access permissions, but they come with a trade-off: each group requires its own spreadsheet import, and you can’t manage all profiles together in one dashboard.

Set the access model before dozens of profiles accumulate different owners:

- **Primary ownership**: Keep this with the business rather than an employee, franchisee, or agency account
- **Group owners**: Limit these to people who need to manage access and ownership
- **Group managers**: Give operational teams the ability to edit profiles without giving them control over users
- **Approval rules**: Decide which changes require review, especially business names, categories, relocations, closures, and major hour changes

[Semrush Listing Management](https://www.semrush.com/listings-management/start/) can sit alongside that workflow. You can connect a Google Business Profile, organize multiple locations under the same brand, and bulk-edit location information.

### Common Google Business Profile mistakes to avoid

- **Duplicate profiles**: Google allows [one Business Profile per eligible business location](https://support.google.com/business/answer/12756178?hl=en); if Google considers a profile a duplicate, it won’t show on Google Search or Maps.
- **Keyword-stuffed names**: Google says the profile name should match the business’s real-world name and prohibits unnecessary information in the name field
- **Incorrect categories**: Choose a [business category](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7249669?hl=en) that describes what the business is. Google recommends categories that complete the statement “This business IS a” rather than “this business HAS a.”
- **Outdated hours**: Keep regular and special hours current so customers aren’t sent to a branch that's closed
- **Ineligible locations**: A Business Profile generally [requires in-person contact](https://support.google.com/business/answer/13763036?hl=en) with customers during stated hours. A rented mailing address where the business doesn’t operate isn’t eligible; an office in a co-working space can qualify when it has clear signage, receives customers during business hours, and is staffed by the business’s own team.

## 7. Handle service-area businesses differently

Service-area businesses need a different Google Business Profile and location-page setup because they travel to customers rather than serving them at a storefront.

Think plumbers, electricians, mobile pet groomers, or home-cleaning companies. If customers don’t visit your business address, Google says you should [hide that address from the Business Profile](https://support.google.com/business/answer/2853879?sjid=17596153839602075776-NC#removegmb) and define the areas you serve instead.

Google lets service-area businesses specify up to 20 service areas by city, postal code, or another geographic area. You can’t define the service area as a radius, and Google says the overall coverage generally [shouldn’t extend beyond about two hours’ driving time](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9157481?hl=en) from the business base.

The number of Google Business Profiles depends on how the business operates:

- **One operating base**: Use one profile with the relevant service areas
- **Several real operating locations**: You can have a profile for each location when each has its own staff and service area
- **Hybrid business**: If customers can visit the location and the business also travels to them, you can show the address and add a service area

### How to scale multi-location SEO for a service-area business

To scale multi-location SEO for a service-area business, use the website to build relevant geographic coverage without creating Google Business Profiles for places where the business has no real operating location.

Say a plumbing company is based in Brooklyn but regularly works in Queens and the Bronx. The company's Google Business Profile can define the areas it serves, while the website can contain useful pages for important markets where there’s real customer activity.

The same standard from step 5 applies here: create a service-area page only when the business has enough genuine local substance to make the page useful on its own. A Queens or Bronx page should reflect real work, customers, coverage, or market-specific information rather than repeat the Brooklyn page with a different place name.

The website and Google Business Profile are doing different jobs here.

The profile represents the real business location and service area. The website gives you room to explain where and how the business operates in much more detail.

A city, therefore, can warrant a local page without warranting another Google Business Profile.

## 8. Add LocalBusiness structured data to each location

Add LocalBusiness structured data to each location page so search engines can identify information about that branch more precisely.

Structured data is standardized code that labels page content for search engines. Google recommends defining each location as a [LocalBusiness type](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/local-business) and using the most specific subtype available, such as Restaurant, DaySpa, or HealthClub.

The [markup](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/intro-structured-data) should describe the same business customers can see on the page.

### Important LocalBusiness properties to consider

Important LocalBusiness properties include two required fields, plus recommended properties that can describe how the individual location operates.

**Required properties**:

- **Name**: The location’s business name
- **Address**: The full physical address

**Recommended properties**:

- **Telephone**: The phone number for that branch
- **Opening hours**: Regular opening and closing times
- **URL**: The corresponding location-page URL
- **Geo coordinates**: Latitude and longitude for the physical location
- **Business-specific properties**: Details such as menu, department, or priceRange where they apply; a department can have its own telephone and openingHours values where applicable.

For multi-location sites, [Semrush’s Site Audit](https://www.semrush.com/siteaudit/) can check structured data across several URLs at once. In Site Audit, open “**Crawled Pages**” and filter by “**Structured data items**.”

Select **“Local Business**” to see which crawled URLs contain LocalBusiness markup. Compare the filtered count with your total number of location pages to spot locations that may be missing the markup.

![Semrush Site Audit advanced filter set to find pages containing Local Business structured data](https://static.semrush.com/blog/uploads/media/b0/db/b0db618a4943081d6ae77404c819aa41/f756840cd97bdc264a963f52323e782b/image.png)

[Semrush’s structured-data report](https://www.semrush.com/kb/1084-structured-data-items-site-audit) shows which pages contain LocalBusiness markup and flags missing required fields, so you can identify locations with incomplete or invalid schema.

## 9. Build trust signals for each individual location

Build trust for each location separately. A strong parent brand helps, but every branch still needs its own local social proof.

Google’s popularity signal considers how well known a business is, including links from other websites and the number of reviews. Google’s documentation [clearly says that](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en#:~:text=More%20reviews%20and%20positive%20ratings%20can%20help%20your%20business%E2%80%99s%20local%20ranking.) “more reviews and positive ratings can help your business’s local ranking.”

AI search widens the evidence base.

### Build a repeatable review strategy

Build a repeatable review strategy by asking customers for feedback at the location level and monitoring how each branch’s review profile develops.

Google allows businesses to ask customers for genuine reviews, but its [rating manipulation policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) prohibits discouraging negative reviews or selectively asking for positive ones.

For each location, monitor:

- **Rating**: The branch’s average review score
- **Volume**: How many reviews it has accumulated
- **Recency**: Whether new customer feedback is arriving consistently
- **Sentiment**: The overall tone of recent reviews
- **Recurring themes**: Services, staff, problems, or strengths customers repeatedly mention

If customers at one branch repeatedly praise evening appointments or complain about parking, that information can improve the location page, FAQs, and the customer experience itself.

[Semrush’s Review Management](https://www.semrush.com/kb/1392-review-management) centralizes reviews from dozens of review sites and lets teams monitor review progress, respond to feedback, and analyze review trends.

For multi-location businesses, use [Groups](https://www.semrush.com/kb/1599-managing-multiple-locations-with-groups) in Review Management to monitor reviews across several locations from one view. The group view shows review progress, total reviews, and average star rating across the selected locations. Use “**View locations**” to drill into individual branches.

![Semrush Review Management dashboard showing 104 reviews and a 4.2 average rating across two locations](https://static.semrush.com/blog/uploads/media/28/73/28731094a03f69760ddf421ddbe07cad/e182eede638ac3e6a69a6355e3856cbc/image.png)

### Earn local links and mentions

Earn links and mentions from organizations that have a real connection to the location.

Look for:

- **Local media**: Coverage of openings, projects, research, or community activity
- **Chambers and associations**: Membership pages and relevant local directories
- **Sponsorships and events**: Organizations the branch supports or takes part in
- **Partners**: Local suppliers, venues, professional partners, or complementary businesses
- **Industry directories**: Reputable directories relevant to the business and market

When a mention or link is about a specific branch, point it to that location page.

For example, Orangetheory’s Port Washington studio [appears in the local Chamber of Commerce directory](https://www.pwcoc.org/members/category/fitness-256) with its own address, phone number, and website.

![Port Washington Chamber of Commerce directory showing an Orangetheory Fitness local business listing](https://static.semrush.com/blog/uploads/media/94/ff/94ff27d941ff47882999757027af7f25/948f5e51af653f3846e23d7e0b9480b0/image.png)

The third-party mentions tie individual locations to the communities they serve rather than only strengthening the parent brand.

## 10. Keep business information consistent across the web

Keep each location’s business information consistent by checking public listings against the approved record you created in Step 1.

### What to do when a location opens, moves, or closes?

When a branch changes its hours, phone number, services, or address, update the central record first. Then use the same information to update or verify the website, Google Business Profile, maps, and other important directories.

- **Opening a location**: Add the approved address, phone number, hours, services, and location-page URL to your source of truth before publishing profiles and listings
- **Moving a location**: Update the central record first, then the location page, Google Business Profile, and priority listings. If the page URL changes, redirect the old URL to the new location page
- **Closing a location**: Update the location’s status across the website, Google Business Profile, and important listings. Remove the branch from active location navigation and decide whether its old URL should redirect to a relevant location hub or nearby branch

[Semrush’s Listing Management](https://www.semrush.com/listings-management/start/) lets you enter or import a location’s information once, distribute it to supported directories, and monitor the listings for differences.

Don’t submit to every directory you can find, though. Focus first on the platforms customers and search engines are most likely to rely on.

### Which listings should you prioritize?

Prioritize listings by reach and relevance. Start with the platforms that apply broadly, then add the directories customers actually use for your category and location.

Start with:

- **Your website and Google Business Profile**: Keep these accurate first because they represent the location directly
- **Major maps and search platforms**: Maintain listings on Apple Maps, Bing, and other widely used discovery platforms
- **Navigation platforms**: Keep location data current on services such as Waze, HERE, and TomTom when customers may use them to reach the business
- **Industry-specific directories**: Focus on platforms customers use for your category, such as WebMD or Zocdoc for healthcare, Angi or Houzz for home services, and OpenTable or Tripadvisor for restaurants
- **Regional and local directories**: Add reputable platforms that are specific to the market, including chambers and established local business directories

The New York City and Houston SERPs from Step 2 show the same pattern. The directories Google surfaces for local searches can help you decide which listings to prioritize in each market.

The right secondary platform can also depend on customer behavior. Generation Tux, for example, added its Chicago, Columbus, and Dallas showrooms to Apple Business Connect after realizing they weren’t appearing in Apple Maps.

In the first month, the company reported a [72% increase in requests](https://business.apple.com/en-ww/success-stories/5579-generation-tux) for showroom directions and a 34% increase in phone calls to physical locations.

## 11. Account for AI-assisted local discovery

Account for AI-assisted local discovery, because customers are already using [large language models (LLMs)](https://www.semrush.com/blog/list-of-large-language-models/) to research local businesses.

In a 2026 Semrush survey, [87.41%](https://www.semrush.com/blog/ai-chatbots-talk-ai-users-out-of-buying/) of people who use AI for company or seller research said they would at least occasionally consult an AI chatbot before hiring a local business. Nearly a third (31.48%) said they would always do so.

And AI answers don’t rely only on businesses’ own websites. In a [2025 Semrush study covering more than 100 million citations](https://www.semrush.com/blog/most-cited-domains-ai/) across 230,000+ prompts, citation patterns varied substantially by platform and changed over time. Reddit, LinkedIn, Wikipedia, YouTube, Forbes, and other third-party domains all appeared among prominent citation sources across ChatGPT Search, Google AI Mode, and Perplexity.

You don’t need a separate local SEO strategy just for AI search. You do, however, need to make it easy to connect accurate information with the right location.

Most of [AI search optimization for local businesses](https://www.semrush.com/blog/ai-search-optimization-for-local-businesses/) is the work this guide already covers, because it gives AI platforms reliable information to retrieve.

### Make location information easy to retrieve and understand

The mechanics are the same ones covered earlier:

- Keep branch data consistent across sources as we covered in Step 1 and Step 10
- Make location pages crawlable and clearly structured in Step 3, Step 4, and Step 8
- Build independent local evidence through reviews and links in Step 9

A consistent database of business information can also help LLMs determine what information is current. Lily Ray, founder of Algorythmic, saw this with her own professional information.

After Ray's job title changed, the old title continued appearing in LLM responses because it still existed across sites that had cited her.

Ray called the cleanup “a lot like NAP consistency in the local SEO space,” at [SEO Week 2025](https://ipullrank.com/seo-week-2025-lily-ray). As she corrected the outdated title across those sources, she found that LLMs became more likely to return the current one.

For a multi-location business, the name, address, services, and other details associated with one location should not contradict the versions appearing elsewhere online.

## 12. Measure SEO performance by location

Measure SEO performance by location instead of relying only on domain-wide traffic and rankings.

A site can look healthy overall while one branch loses visibility, another gains it, and a third gets traffic that never turns into calls or bookings. Use the same reporting structure for every location so you can compare markets consistently.

Track four layers of local SEO performance:

- **Search visibility**: Organic rankings and local pack visibility
- **Website performance**: Impressions, clicks, sessions, and conversions for each location page
- **Google Business Profile activity**: Views, calls, directions, website clicks, and bookings where available
- **Business outcomes**: Leads, appointments, revenue, or pipeline tied to the location

### Track rankings for a multi-location site

Track rankings for each location in the market it serves.

### Track local actions and business outcomes

## 13. Prioritize locations instead of treating every branch equally

Prioritize locations based on where SEO can have the greatest business impact. Every location should meet the same baseline: accurate business information, a crawlable location page, an eligible Google Business Profile, and ongoing review and listing management.

Beyond that baseline, use the location-level data you’ve collected throughout this process to decide where to invest more.

Look at:

- **Search demand**: How much relevant local demand exists in the market
- **Current visibility**: How well the location already performs in organic search and Google Maps
- **Business value**: Revenue, lead value, capacity, margins, or other commercial priorities for that branch
- **Growth potential**: Whether the market has room to gain customers or expand services

For example, high search demand doesn’t automatically make a location the top SEO priority. If the branch already dominates its important queries and is operating near capacity, another market with weaker visibility may offer a better use of your SEO budget.

Use [Keyword Magic Tool](https://www.semrush.com/analytics/keywordmagic/) for demand, [Position Tracking](https://www.semrush.com/position-tracking/) and [Map Rank Tracker](https://www.semrush.com/map-rank-tracker/) for visibility, and [Review Management](https://www.semrush.com/kb/1392-review-management) and [Listing Management](https://www.semrush.com/listings-management/start/) for gaps in local trust and business information.

Then group locations into tiers and define what each tier receives. A tiering model could look like this:

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tier** | **What does it look like?** | **SEO priority** |
| Growth | Strong demand and business potential, but weak current visibility | Invest heavily in content, links, reviews, and local visibility |
| Defend | Strong visibility in an important market | Protect rankings, keep information accurate, and watch for losses |
| Test | Promising market, but the likely return is less certain | Make selective improvements and measure whether they produce results |
| Maintenance | Lower commercial priority or limited local demand | Maintain the SEO baseline and fix issues as they appear |

Review the tiers on a set cadence, ideally quarterly. A Growth location can become a Defend location once visibility improves. A Test market can earn more investment if rankings begin turning into leads or revenue.

## Your multi-location SEO checklist

A scalable multi-location SEO strategy gives every location the same foundation, while leaving room for the differences that make each market local.

Use the checklist below as a recurring operating standard:

**Accurate data**:

- Maintain one approved record for each location’s NAP, hours, services, URLs, categories, and status
- Update the central record first when location information changes
- Check that updates reach your website, Google Business Profile, schema, maps, and priority directories
- Keep ownership and approval rules documented

**Discoverable pages**:

- Give every real location a dedicated, crawlable page
- Keep location pages connected to the central Locations or Areas We Serve hub
- Link each branch to the services available there
- Add genuinely local information rather than changing only the city name
- Review location pages for crawlability, duplication, structured data, and other technical issues

**Local profiles**:

- Maintain an eligible Google Business Profile for each real location
- Keep hours, categories, services, photos, links, and conversion actions current
- Link profiles to the corresponding location page
- Review profile access so ownership doesn’t depend on individual employees or agencies

**Independent trust**:

- Ask for genuine reviews at the location level
- Monitor rating, volume, recency, sentiment, and recurring feedback by branch
- Keep important third-party listings accurate
- Build links, citations, and mentions that reflect each location’s real presence in its market

**Location-level measurement**:

- Track organic and Maps visibility for each market
- Monitor location-page impressions, clicks, sessions, and conversions
- Track calls, directions, bookings, leads, and revenue where possible
- Compare locations using the same KPIs and reporting periods
- Revisit location tiers as demand, visibility, and business priorities change
