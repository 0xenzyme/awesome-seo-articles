# 10 SEO Myths You Should Ignore

Most myths are harmless. Gum takes seven years to digest. Sharks don’t get cancer. Lightning never strikes the same place twice.

Unfortunately, the same can’t be said for most SEO myths. At best, they’re slightly misleading. At worst, they lead you to waste precious time, money, and resources on things that will never [improve SEO](https://ahrefs.com/blog/how-to-improve-seo/).

So let’s bust a few common SEO myths once and for all.

1. [SEO is dead](#seo-is-dead)
2. [SEO is a one-time thing](#seo-is-a-one-off)
3. [Google only ranks ‘fresh’ content](#google-only-ranks-fresh-content)
4. [Long-tail keywords are easier to rank for](#long-tail-keywords-are-easier)
5. [Duplicate content will get you penalized](#duplicate-content-penalty)
6. [Social signals help rankings](#social-signals-help-rankings)
7. [PPC can’t help you rank higher](#ppc-cant-help-rankings)
8. [PageRank doesn’t matter anymore](#pagerank-doesnt-matter)
9. [SEO is all about rankings](#seo-is-all-about-rankings)
10. [Keyword research isn’t important](#keyword-research-isnt-important)

## 1. SEO is dead

Perhaps this one is more straight nonsense than a myth, but it’s worth putting to bed nonetheless.

Lazy journalism likes to proclaim many things dead regularly, and SEO is no different. According to Ahrefs’ [Content Explorer](https://ahrefs.com/content-explorer), this phrase has been uttered 3,367 times since June 2016.

On average, that’s 66 times a month.

So let’s set the record straight once and for all:

**SEO. Is. Not. Dead.**

How do we know? Well, here’s the organic search traffic to our blog over the past three months:

The truth is that as long as [search engines](https://ahrefs.com/blog/alternative-search-engines/) still exist, are used, and show organic results that you can influence in some way, then SEO isn’t dead.

## 2. SEO is a one-time thing

SEO is a bit like going to the gym. It’s okay to miss a workout here and there, but things soon start to fall apart if you stay at home eating chips too often. (Trust me, I know.)

Here’s what it looks like when you start neglecting your SEO:

Before this period, I was actively optimizing the site. Then, I neglected it and shifted my attention to other things. You can see that traffic slowly declined over the following months.

There are several reasons this can happen:

- Your competitors overtake you (by consistently working on their SEO).
- You start losing [backlinks](https://ahrefs.com/blog/what-are-backlinks/) due to ‘link rot.’
- Your content loses ‘freshness’ (more on this shortly).

That’s why [most SEO professionals (74.71%) charge a monthly retainer](https://ahrefs.com/blog/seo-pricing/) for their services, rather than an hourly rate or one-time fee. They know SEO is an ongoing process and that their clients need to invest long-term to see results.

Is this true for absolutely all sites? No. There are rare instances where a site can be neglected for years and continue to pull in consistent traffic month after month.

## 3. Google only ranks ‘fresh’ content

[Republishing content](https://ahrefs.com/blog/republishing-content/) is something we do a lot. If we filter for republished posts on the blog in Ahrefs’ [Content Explorer](https://ahrefs.com/content-explorer), there are 136 since 2016.

So why are we investing so much effort into keeping our content fresh if freshness is a myth?

‘Freshness’ is a query-dependent [ranking factor](https://ahrefs.com/blog/google-ranking-factors/). That means it matters more for some queries than others. It all depends on whether the freshness of the content has any impact on content quality.

SEO is continually evolving, so freshness matters for many SEO-related queries.

You can see that traffic was declining for months, but then recovered pretty much overnight when we refreshed and republished it. That’s because the top Google searches change all the time, so people expect ‘fresh’ results.

On the flip side, freshness doesn’t particularly matter for a query like “how to tie a tie” because the process never changes. That’s why Google is quite happy to rank pages in the top three that were last updated in 2013.

## 4. Long-tail keywords are easier to rank for

Most people think [long-tail keywords](https://ahrefs.com/blog/long-tail-keywords/) consist of many words, but this isn’t entirely accurate. Long-tail keywords are queries with low individual search volumes. The number of words doesn’t matter.

## 5. Duplicate content will get you penalized

[Duplicate content](https://ahrefs.com/blog/duplicate-content/) is where exact or near-duplicate content shows up in more than one place. It can be on the same website or across multiple sites.

For example, both of these URLs take you to the same blog post:

`https://buffer.com/library/social-media-manager-checklist/`
 `https://buffer.com/resources/social-media-manager-checklist/`

Google has said there’s no duplicate content penalty [on](https://twitter.com/methode/status/831047566284431360) [numerous](https://webmasters.googleblog.com/2008/09/demystifying-duplicate-content-penalty.html) [occasions](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s7kP7LWM87Y&feature=youtu.be&t=6m33s&ab_channel=GoogleWebmasters), but this myth still gets peddled to oblivion on the regular.

However, while duplicate content won’t get you penalized, it can cause SEO issues such as:

- Undesirable or unfriendly URLs in search results;
- Backlink dilution;
- Wasted [crawl budget](https://ahrefs.com/blog/crawl-budget/);
- Scraped or [syndicated content](https://ahrefs.com/blog/content-syndication/) outranking you.

If you’re concerned that you might have duplicate content on your site, run a crawl with Ahrefs’ [Site Audit](https://ahrefs.com/site-audit) and check the **Duplicate content** report. If you see clusters of near or exact duplicates, it may be worth fixing the issues.

Learn how to do that in [our beginner’s guide to duplicate content](https://ahrefs.com/blog/duplicate-content/).

## 6. Social signals help rankings

It’s not farfetched to assume that the more your content gets shared on sites like Facebook, Twitter, and Pinterest, the higher it’ll rank. After all, if tons of people share something, it must be interesting and valuable and worthy of ranking, right?

Maybe, but [according to Google’s John Mueller](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WszvyRune14#t=1192), social signals don’t directly impact rankings.

That’s probably because social signals are so stupendously easy to manipulate. Seriously, take a look on Fiverr. You can get thousands of them for a few bucks.

But if this is true, why do many [studies](https://cognitiveseo.com/blog/11903/social-signals-seo-influence/) show a correlation between social shares and rankings?

There’s no definitive reason for this, but we think these are the two most significant contributing factors:

1. **More shares lead to more exposure**, and that often leads to more backlinks (which are a ranking factor).
2. **Pages that rank well in Google get more traffic**, and some of those people will share the content on social media.

## 7. PPC can’t help you rank higher

Let’s be clear: paying Google for ads won’t **directly** influence rankings. I love conspiracy theories as much as the next person, but Google isn’t going to rank you higher because you’re lining their pockets.

However, that doesn’t mean PPC can’t **indirectly** help you rank higher.

And that’s because [PPC ads](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ppc-marketing/) can help attract backlinks.

Don’t believe us? We recently spent $1,246 on Google ads with the intention of [building links](https://ahrefs.com/seo/link-building) to one of our blog posts. The result? Backlinks from eleven new websites.

## 9. SEO is all about rankings

Everyone wants to rank number one, but that’s usually because they assume that the number one result gets the most traffic. However, this isn’t always true. Our [study](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ranking-number-one-is-overrated/) of 100,000 search queries shows that the top-ranking page only gets the most search traffic 49% of the time.

Why? Because most pages get traffic from many keywords, not just one.

For example, look at the top-ranking pages for “high protein diet.” The page in position two gets more estimated traffic than the page in position one because it ranks for more keywords with search demand.

The lesson here is that it’s time to stop agonizing over first-place rankings and start focusing on traffic instead.

You can read more about how to do that [here](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ranking-number-one-is-overrated/), but the basics are:

1. Cover your topic in more depth.
2. Build more backlinks to boost page-level “authority.”
3. Nail [search intent](https://ahrefs.com/blog/search-intent/).
